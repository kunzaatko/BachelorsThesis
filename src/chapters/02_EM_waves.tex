\section{Historical Context}%
\label{historical-context}

Light was perceived in many different ways throughout the history of human kind.

The first major work that studied optics was written by \nameemph{Euclid} in the 4\textsuperscript{th} century BCE.
It mainly dealt with the geometry of vision and did not study the physical aspects of light in much detail. 
However his views of light and vision were significantly different from ours\sidenote{%
        For instance, he conjectured that vision is formed by rays emanating from the observers eyes.

Optics as a field is often considered to have been commenced by \nameemph{Ḥasan Ibn al-Haytham}\sidenote{%

    } in the 11\textsuperscript{th} century by his experimental work and 
    the \emph{Book of Optics}, which he wrote to summarize them.
It introduced the idea that vision results from light entering our eyes through the retina as opposed to the other way
    around\sidenote{This was further developed by \nameemph{Johannes Kepler} in the 17\textsuperscript{th} century, who 
    suggested the concept of retinal images.}.
He also extensively studied the \emph{camera obscura} phenomenon in his treatise \emph{On the Shape of the Eclipse} in
    which he ventured to explain the crescent shaped images formed during an eclipse when light passes through a small 
    aperture as can be seen in \reffig{crescent-shaped-image} \sidecite{raynaud2016}.

There have been many attempts to explain light during the middle ages, but more often than not, they were not in 
    accordance with its known experimental properties.
% TODO: Reference (unspecified??) <08-11-23> 
In particular, a notable idea was brought forward by \nameemph{René Descartes} in the beginning of the
    17\textsuperscript{th} century, who described light as a propagating pressure in an unspecified medium\sidenote{This
        view has interestingly close properties to the way, light is described in present day optics.}. 
In 1690 \nameemph{Christiaan Huygens} introduces the \emph{wave theory of light} in his work \emph{Traité de la
    Lumière}.
His major contribution was the conceptualization of light as a vibration in a medium and his methods of analysis are 
    used even today termed as the \emph{Huygens-Fresnel principle}.
The first comprehensive theory of light that was widely adopted, can be dated back to \nameemph{Sir Isaac Newton} at the
    start of the 18\textsuperscript{th} century. %  with regards to the physical reality around us, that was developed in parallel with experimentation
\nameemph{Newton} was aware of the wave theory of light but rejected it in favour of the \emph{corpuscular theory}, 
    which he laid the foundations to\sidecite{newton1704}.
It states that light is composed of particles called \emph{corpuscles} travelling in a straight line with finite
    velocity.
By the rise of the corpuscular theory, the branch that we now call \emph{geometrical optics}, which was earlier 
    developed by \nameemph{Alhazen} was markedly improved.
The corpuscular theory and geometrical optics explained a mayor part of the conundrums that astronomers had about the 
    optical instrumentation of that time and due to this it prevailed for a long time at the forefront of established 
    theories of light.

However, for the recognition of the way light passes through and transforms in a microscope, or in fact any optical 
    system that we use for observation, and especially to study the resolution that is possible to achieve, it is
    necessary to advance to the later centuries and developments made in the wave 
    theory of light.
A radical shift towards the supremacy of the wave model explanation instead of light rays, was the famous 
    \emph{double-slit experiment} performed by \nameemph{Thomas Young} in 1801, which leads to results that are in
    conflict with the sole explanation by the simpler corpuscular theory.
Further mathematical structure to the notion of interference emerging from the double-slit experiment was given by 
    \nameemph{Augustin-Jean Fresnel}, who in 1818 was the first to give a satisfactory explanation of diffraction by a 
    straight edge in the form of the \emph{Fresnel integral}, which will be important for us in further sections.
The brilliant mathematician \nameemph{James Clerk Maxwell} later in 1873 gave a unified and robust theoretical framework
    for \ac{EM} fields, which light is a manifestation of.

\section[Wave Theory of Light]{Foundations of the Wave Theory of Light}%
\labsec{wave-theory-of-light}
% TODO: Explanations of ∇ × and ∇ ⋅ in the margin with Einstein's summation (nabla is also called del...)<06-01-24> 

Maxwell's unification of the theory of \ac{EM} fields came in the manner of twenty differential equations which were 
    later condensed by \nameemph{Oliver Heaviside} into the notorious forms of the four equations that are today known 
    as \emph{Maxwell's equations}.
Given in their most general differential form in \acs{SI} units, they are
    \begin{align}
        \nabla \cdot \permittivity \E &= \rho, \tag{\text{Gauss's law for \(\E\)}}\labeq{gausses-law-E}\\ 
        \nabla \cdot \permeability \B &= 0, \tag{\text{Gauss's law for \(\B\)}}\labeq{gausses-law-B}\\  
        \nabla \times \E &= -\pdif{t}\B, \tag{\text{Faraday's law}} \labeq{faradays-law} \\
        \nabla \times \B &= \permeability \J  + \permittivity \permeability \pdif{t}\E, \tag{\text{Ampère-Maxwell's law}} \labeq{amperes-law}
    \end{align}
    where \(\E=\E(\vec{r}, t)\) and \(\B=\B(\vec{r}, t)\) are the electric and magnetic fields respectively, 
    \(\J=\J(\vec{r}, t)\) is the current density, \(\rho = \rho(\vec{r})\) is the electric charge density, \(\permittivity = 
    \permittivity(\vec{r})\) and \(\permeability = \permeability(\vec{r})\) are permittivity and permeability of the 
    propagation medium respectively.
These equations can be simplified for the case of the medium that is applicable to us \sidecite{goodman2005c}.
As a first simplification, we assume the absence of free charges (\(\rho \equiv 0\)) and free currents (\(\J \equiv 0\)) 
    which lead to the simplification of \refeqraw{gausses-law-E} and \refeqraw{amperes-law}.
We postulate a well-behaved \emph{insulating} (\emph{dielectric}) medium in its response to the electric field, where 
    \(\D = \permittivity \E + \Pol\) is the electric displacement field and \(\Pol\) is the field of polarization, in 
    the form of an assumption of \emph{linearity} implying that the polarization \(\Pol\) is proportional to the 
    electric field, that is \(\Pol \propto \E\), \emph{non-dispersiveness} meaning that the polarization \(\Pol(t)\) is 
    dependent on \(\E(t)\) only at the same time instant, \emph{local homogeneity} meaning that the dependence of 
    \(\Pol\) on \(\E\) is the same at every point \(\vec{r}\) within a neighbourhood and \emph{isotropy} meaning that 
    the relation is identical for every direction of \(\E\). 
In the terms of a medium within which the light propagates \emph{non-dispersiveness} implies that
    \({\permittivity(\lambda) \equiv\text{const.}}\) where \(\lambda\) is the wavelength of the propagating \ac{EM} wave,
    \emph{homogeneity} implies \({\permittivity(\vec{r}) \equiv \text{const.}}\) in some neighbourhood and \emph{isotropy}
    implies that \({\permittivity({\E}/{|\E|}) \equiv \text{const.}}\) so in all directions of the field, the permitivity is
    the same.
Also the medium in optics is assumed to be \emph{non-magnetic} and so \(\permeability \equiv \permeability_0\), which is
    the permeability in vacuum.
The applicable simplified equations are
    \begin{equation}
        \labeq{maxwell-simplified}
        \begin{aligned}
            \nabla \cdot  \E &= 0, &   \nabla \cdot  \B &= 0, \\  
            \nabla \times \E &= -\pdif{t}\B, & \nabla \times \B &= \permittivity \permeability_0 \pdif{t}\E.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
Notice that due to the \emph{homogeneity}, the permittivity \(\permittivity\) and permeability \(\permeability\), no
    longer appear in Gauss's law for \(\E\) and \(\B\) in the simplified equations.

\subsection[Wave Equation of Light]{The Wave Equation for Light}
\labsubsec{wave-equation}

The goal is to transform the equations of \refeqraw{maxwell-simplified} to the wave equation for both \(\E\) and \(\B\)
% TODO: Note about the vector laplacian operator ∇² and the normal laplacian operator <06-01-24> 
    \begin{equation*}
        \nabla^2 \wave - \frac{\pdif[order={2}]{t}\wave}{v^2} = 0,
        \labeq{wave-equation}\tag{\(\dAlembert_{v}\wave\)}
    \end{equation*}
    where \(v\) is the waves propagation speed.
For \(\E\), this can be done starting from Faraday's law in the simplified form~(\refeqshort{maxwell-simplified}) by 
    applying the curl operation to both sides~\sidecite{feynman2011a}.
Customarily, starting from Faraday's law, we get for the left side
    \begin{equation*}
      \nabla \times (\nabla \times \E) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\E) - \nabla^2\E = - \nabla^2\E,
    \end{equation*}
    where we used, in order, the \emph{curl of curl} identity and Gauss's law for \(\E\) and for the right side
    \begin{equation*}
        -\nabla\times(\pdif{t}\B) = - \pdif{t}(\nabla \times \B) = - \pdif{t}(\permittivity
        \permeability_0 \pdif{t} \E) = - \permittivity \permeability_0 \pdif[order={2}]{t} \E,
    \end{equation*}
    where we used the fact that the \(\pdif{t}\) operation is scalar (does not introduce ties between the positional
    dimensions) and therefore the order of operations can be swapped and Ampère-Maxwell's law.
% TODO: Here include the relation of double rot in the margin proved <06-01-24> 
Putting these two results together gives the wave equation for \(\E\)
    \begin{equation}
        \nabla^2\E - \permittivity \permeability_0 \pdif[order={2}]{t}\E = 0 \tag{\(\dAlembert\E\)}\labeq{wave-equation-E}. 
    \end{equation}
An analogous sequence of operations leads to the wave equation for~\(\B\)
    \begin{equation}
        \nabla^2\B - \permittivity \permeability_0 \pdif[order={2}]{t}\B = 0 \tag{\dAlembert\B}\labeq{wave-equation-B}.
    \end{equation}

Comparing the general \(n\)-dimensional wave equation (\refeqraw{wave-equation}) with the wave equations 
    \refeqraw{wave-equation-E} and \refeqraw{wave-equation-B}, we can determine the propagation speed~\(v\) of the 
    \ac{EM} wave in the medium as
    \begin{equation}
           \permittivity \permeability_0 = 1/v^2 \implies v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0}}.
    \end{equation}
This is in accordance with the known value of the speed of light in vacuum \(c
    = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\permittivity_0\permeability_0}} = \qty{299 792 458}{\meter/\second}\).


\section[Wave Equation Solutions]{Solutions to the Wave Equation of Light}
\labsec{solutions-wave-equation}

There are two solutions to \refeq{wave-equation} that will be of interest to us.
They differ by the shape of the wave-front surface that is produced.
First we have the \emph{spherical wave}, that is more aligned with the apprehension of light sources in \ac{FM} in
    theory theory as will become apparent.
There is a problem with the spherical wave solution that will be demonstrated, hence, the \emph{planar wave} solution 
    will be also derived.

\subsection{Spherical Wave}
\labsubsec{spherical-wave}

Let us consider a solution \(\wavescal\) to the \(3\)-dimensional wave equation (\refeqraw{wave-equation}) that is dependent
    only on the radial distance from the origin \({r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}}\) and time~\(t\).
The Laplace operator \(\nabla^2\) of \(\wavescal\) gives us
    \begin{align*}
        \nabla^2\wavescal &= \sum_{i = 1}^{3} \pdv[order={2}]{\wavescal(r)}{x_i}  = \sum_{i = 1}^{3}
        \pdv{}{x_i}\ab(\pdv{\wavescal(r)}{x_i}) = \sum_{i = 1}^{3} \pdv{}{x_i}\ab(\pdv{\wavescal(r)}{r}\pdv{r}{x_i}) \\
                      &= \sum_{i = 1}^{3} \pdv{}{x_i}\ab(\pdv{\wavescal(r)}{r}\frac{x_i}{r}) 
                      = \sum_{i = 1}^{3} \frac{x_i}{r} \pdv{\wavescal(r)}{x_i, r}
                      + \pdv{}{x_i}\ab(\frac{x_i}{r})\pdv{\wavescal(r)}{r} \\
                      &= \sum_{i = 1}^{3} \ab(\frac{x_i}{r})^2 \pdv[order={2}]{\wavescal(r)}{r} + \frac{1}{r}\ab(1
                      - \frac{x_i^2}{r^2}) \pdv{\wavescal(r)}{r} = \pdv[order={2}]{\wavescal(r)}{r}
        + \frac{2}{r}\pdv{\wavescal(r)}{r} \\
                      &= \frac{1}{r}\pdv[order={2}]{}{r}(r\wavescal(r)).
    \end{align*}
If rewrite the wave equation with this result and expand both sides by \(r\), we get 
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{1}{r}\pdv[order={2}]{}{r}(r\wavescal) - \frac{1}{v^2}\pdv[order={2}]{\wavescal}{t} &= 0, \\
        \pdv[order={2}]{}{r}(r\wavescal) - \frac{1}{v^2}\pdv[order={2}]{r\wavescal}{t} &= 0, \\
        \nabla^2(r\wavescal) - \frac{1}{v^2}\pdv[order={2}]{r\wavescal}{t} &= 0, \\
    \end{align*}
    which is the wave equation (\refeqraw{wave-equation}) in one dimension for the function~\(r\wavescal\).
The solution to the one-dimensional wave equation is in the form the d'Alembert's solution
    \begin{equation}
        \tilde{\wavescal}(r,t) = \phi_{\rightarrow}(r - vt) + \phi_{\leftarrow}(r + vt). \labeq{d-alembert-solution-1d}
        \tag{d'Alembert's solution}
    \end{equation}
Of the two solutions, only the outwards propagated component \(\phi_{\rightarrow}\) makes physical sense for a point 
    source generating the spherical wave, so in the standard result, \(\phi_{\leftarrow}\) is omitted by setting it to a zero function 
    \(\phi_{\leftarrow} \equiv 0\).
The final spherical solution \(\wavescal_{\circ}\) for light at the point \(\vec{r}=(x,y,z)\) is
    \begin{equation}
        \wavescal_\circ(\vec{r},t) = \frac{\phi_{\rightarrow}(|\vec{r}| - vt)}{|\vec{r}|}.
    \end{equation}

One consideration is left to be discussed about this solution before reinterpreting it for the \ac{EM} field.
As you may have noticed in this section, the notation of \(\wavescal\) has been used instead of \(\wave\).
Before pronouncing the solution as a \ac{EM} field solution, it is imperative to verify the consistency of it with
    Maxwell's equations, because it is implied that the solution to Maxwell's equations are also \ac{EM} wave equation 
    solutions, but the inverse is not true.
In fact this solution is a counter example to the inverse implication, since due to Birkhoff's theorem, this solution is
    in conflict with source-free Maxwell's equations while assuming that the solution is \emph{non-static}, which means 
    \(v \neq 0\) in the terms of the derived \(\wavescal_\circ\).
To see this, let us start with the ansatz of a spherically symmetric field
    \begin{gather*}
    \E(\vec{r}, t) = \Escal(r,t) \unitvec{r}, \\
    \B(\vec{r}, t) = \Bscal(r,t) \unitvec{r},
    \end{gather*}
    where \(\unitvec{r}\) is the unit vector in the direction of \(\vec{r}\).
Plugging this into the right sides of Faraday's and Ampère-Maxwell's equations gives
    \begin{gather*}
        \nabla \times \E = \nabla \times \Escal(r,t) \unitvec{r} = \vec{0}, \\
        \nabla \times \B = \nabla \times \Bscal(r,t) \unitvec{r} = \vec{0},
    \end{gather*}
    hence the left sides follow as
    \begin{gather*}
    - \pdif{t}\B = \vec{0}, \\
    \permittivity\permeability_0\pdif{t}\E = \vec{0},
    \end{gather*}
    so the field is necessarily static for non-zero \(\permittivity\) and \(\permeability\), which is the only physical
    possibility for these quantities.
It may be argued that a static solution, is also a possible solution, but it must be realized, that a static field
    implies an energy flux of zero and therefore cannot be a manifestation of light since no energy is transmitted.

A thought that we went astray with this solutions derivation is wrong however.
Even though the solution is non-physical, it is widely used in optics, especially in diffraction theory, as an
    approximation to the true physical solution to avoid analytical dead-ends in the calculations.
To this purpose, in conclusion, the approximation of the \(\E\) component of the \ac{EM} field solution is
    \begin{equation*}
      \Escal_{\circ}(\vec{r},t)  = \frac{F\ab(|\vec{r}| - \frac{t}{\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0}})}{|r|},
    \end{equation*}
    where \(F \coloneqq \phi_{\rightarrow}\) is a chosen function.
An analogous expression is available for \(\B\).

The interesting observation to be made is that the intensity of the \ac{EM} field declines in an inverse proportion to
    the radial distance \(r\) from the source.
This can be intuitively realized by considering the fact that the wave-front surface is growing in a quadratic relation 
    with distance \(r\) and the energy transmitted by the wave is also in a quadratic relation to \(\Escal\).
Since the medium is non-dispersive, energy is not lost during the transmission and \(\Escal^2 \cdot S_{\text{wave-front}}
    \propto 1(r)\).

As for the adequacy of the spherical solution compared to the planar wave solution, we can conceptualize the source of
    radiation in \ac{FM} as an electron of a fluorescent molecule in an excited state falling to the ground state while
    releasing a photon of a particular wavelength.
Due to the small size of an atom with the dimensionality in the order of \(\qty{1}{\angstrom}\) relative to the
    dimensionality of the field quantities such as the wavelength in the order of \(\qty{100}{\nano\meter}\), the source
    of the field may be imagined as a point source, which is consistent with the imagination of the source, causing
    a spherical wave.


\subsection{Planar Wave}
\labsubsec{planar-wave}

For the development of a plane wave, we can benefit by starting with the solution to the one-dimensional wave 
    (\refeqraw{d-alembert-solution-1d}).
Let us define the unit vector \(\unitvec{n}\) of direction of propagation of the \(\E\)-wave (\(|\unitvec{n}| = 1\)) and
    the \emph{wave vector} \(\vec{k} = k \unitvec{n}\).
Next let us rewrite Gausses law for \(\E\) (\refeqshort{maxwell-simplified}) in the orthogonal basis 
    \((\unitvec{n}, \unitvec{n}_{\perp_1}, \unitvec{n}_{\perp_2})\)
    \begin{equation}
        \labeq{unitvec-basis-gauss-E}
        \nabla \cdot \E = \pdif{\unitvec{n}}(\E)_{\unitvec{n}} + \pdif{\unitvec{n}_{\perp_1}}(\E)_{\unitvec{n}_{\perp_1}}
        + \pdif{\unitvec{n}_{\perp_2}}(\E)_{\unitvec{n}_{\perp_2}} = 0,
    \end{equation}
    where \(\pdif{\unitvec{v}}\) is the spatial partial derivative in the direction of \(\unitvec{v}\) and 
    \((\E)_{\unitvec{v}}\) is the basis vector \(\unitvec{v}\) component of \(\E\).
The assumption that the wave is propagating in the direction \(\unitvec{n}\) implies
    \begin{gather*}
        \pdif{\unitvec{n}_{\perp_1}}(\E)_{\unitvec{n}_{\perp_1}} = 0, \\
        \pdif{\unitvec{n}_{\perp_2}}(\E)_{\unitvec{n}_{\perp_2}} = 0,
    \end{gather*}
    which when plugged into \refeq{unitvec-basis-gauss-E} in turn implies
    \begin{equation*}
        \pdif{\unitvec{n}}(\E)_{\unitvec{n}} = 0.
    \end{equation*}
If we now consider Ampère-Maxwell's law of \refeq{maxwell-simplified} and again assume that the \(\B\)-wave is
    propagating in the direction of \(\unitvec{n}\), meaning
    \begin{gather*}
        \pdif{\unitvec{n}_{\perp_1}}(\B)_{\unitvec{n}_{\perp_1}} = 0, \\
        \pdif{\unitvec{n}_{\perp_2}}(\B)_{\unitvec{n}_{\perp_2}} = 0,
    \end{gather*}
    and therefore
    \begin{equation*}
        (\nabla \times \B)_{\unitvec{n}} = 0,
    \end{equation*}
    so we obtain that \((\E)_{\unitvec{n}}\) is constant in time.
This implies, that is we assume a dynamic \(\E\)-field, \((\E)_{\unitvec{n}} \equiv 0\).
To put this into words, we can say that for the propagation of planar waves in the direction \(\unitvec{n}\), \(\E\) is
    perpendicular to the direction of propagation, \(\E \perp \unitvec{n}\).
Since we know that \(\E\) lies in the plane \(\unitvec{n}_{\perp_1} \times \unitvec{n}_{\perp_2}\), we can denote the
    direction \({\E/|\E| \eqqcolon\unitvec{m}}\) and change reinterpret \(\E\) in the basis 
    \((\unitvec{n}, \unitvec{m}, \unitvec{x}_{\perp})\).
The calculation of \(\nabla \times \E\) is now trivial, as the only non-zero component of \(\E\) is 
    \((\E)_{\unitvec{m}}\) and so
    \begin{gather*}
        (\nabla\times\E)_{\unitvec{n}}  =  (\nabla\times\E)_{\unitvec{m}} = 0,\\
        (\nabla\times\E)_{\unitvec{x}_{\perp}}  =  \pdif{\unitvec{n}}(\E)_{\unitvec{m}}.
    \end{gather*}
Now we can see that if \(\E\) is in the direction of \(\unitvec{m}\) then substituting the curl of \(\E\) into Faraday's
    law (\refeqshort{maxwell-simplified})
    \begin{gather*}
        \pdif{t} (\B)_{\unitvec{m}} =  \pdif{t} (\B)_{\unitvec{m}} = 0, \\
        \pdif{t} (\B)_{\unitvec{x}_{\perp}} =  \pdif{\unitvec{n}} (\E)_{\unitvec{m}}, \\
    \end{gather*}
    and we can again leverage the assumption of a dynamic \(\B\)-field and conclude that if the wave propagates in the
    direction \(\unitvec{n}\), then the direction of the \(\E\)-field is \(\unitvec{m}\) and the direction of the
    \(\B\)-field is the perpendicular \(\unitvec{x}_{\perp}\).
In summary \(\E \perp \B \perp \unitvec{n}\).
The same argument can be used to determine the curl \(\nabla \times \B\) which results in the singular non-zero
    component 
    \begin{equation*}
        (\nabla \times \B)_{\unitvec{m}} = \pdif{\unitvec{n}}(\B)_{\unitvec{x}_{\perp}} = \permittivity\permeability_0
        \pdif{t}(\E)_{\unitvec{m}}.
    \end{equation*}
We have arrived at two equations that tie the \(\E\)-field and \(\B\)-field together
    \begin{align}
        \pdif{t} (\B)_{\unitvec{x}_{\perp}} &=  \pdif{\unitvec{n}} (\E)_{\unitvec{m}},\labeq{diftBdifnE} \\
        \pdif{\unitvec{n}}(\B)_{\unitvec{x}_{\perp}} &= \permittivity\permeability_0 \pdif{t}(\E)_{\unitvec{m}}.
        \labeq{difnBdiftE}
    \end{align}
To put this all together and finish the planar wave solution we only need to apply \(\pdif{\unitvec{n}}\) to 
    \refeq{diftBdifnE} and \(\pdif{t}\) to \refeq{difnBdiftE} and observe that the right sides of both equations are 
    identical implying the equality of the left sides
    \begin{equation}
        \labeq{plane-wave-E}
        \pdif[order={2}]{\unitvec{n}}(\E)_{\unitvec{m}} - \permittivity\permeability_0 \pdif[order={2}]{t}(\E)_{\unitvec{m}} = 0.
    \end{equation}
If we make the analogous last step for the \(\B\)-field, we get
    \begin{equation}
        \labeq{plane-wave-B}
        \pdif[order={2}]{\unitvec{n}}(\B)_{\unitvec{x}_{\perp}} - \permittivity\permeability_0 \pdif[order={2}]{t}(\B)_{\unitvec{x}_{\perp}} = 0.
    \end{equation}

Observing the equations \refeq{plane-wave-E} and \refeq{plane-wave-B}, it is clear that they are one-dimensional wave
    equations and the \refeqraw{d-alembert-solution-1d}, can be used to specify the form of the fields
    \begin{gather*}
        (\E(\vec{r}, t))_{\unitvec{m}} = \phi^{\E}_{\rightarrow}\ab((\vec{r})_{\unitvec{n}}  - \frac{t}{\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0}})
        + \phi^{\E}_{\leftarrow}\ab((\vec{r})_{\unitvec{n}} + \frac{t}{\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0}}) \\
        (\B(\vec{r}, t))_{\unitvec{x}_{\perp}} = \phi^{\B}_{\rightarrow}\ab((\vec{r})_{\unitvec{n}}  - \frac{t}{\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0}})
        + \phi^{\B}_{\leftarrow}\ab((\vec{r})_{\unitvec{n}} + \frac{t}{\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0}}).
    \end{gather*}
Based on empirical physical properties of the wave, in contemporary physics, we omit \(\phi_{\leftarrow}\) and are left
    with the retarded time solutions
    \begin{gather}
        \labeq{E-plane-wave}(\E(\vec{r}, t))_{\unitvec{m}} = \phi^{\E}\ab((\vec{r})_{\unitvec{n}}  - \frac{t}{\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0}}), \\
        \labeq{B-plane-wave}(\B(\vec{r}, t))_{\unitvec{x}_{\perp}} = \phi^{\B}\ab((\vec{r})_{\unitvec{n}}  - \frac{t}{\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0}}).
    \end{gather}

To clean up the notation, we restore the standard Cartesian coordinate system as 
    \((\unitvec{n}, \unitvec{m}, \unitvec{x}_{\perp}) = (\unitvec{x}, \unitvec{y}, \unitvec{z})\) and \(\E = (\Escal_x,
    \Escal_y, \Escal_z)\), \(\B = (\Bscal_x, \Bscal_y, \Bscal_z)\) and denote the retarded \(x\)-coordinate 
    \(x - \frac{t}{\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0}}\) as \(\xi\).

This is not the final result though, since se can determine the relation of \(\phi^{\E}\) and \(\phi^{\B}\) by using one
    of the established equations \refeqraw{diftBdifnE} and \refeqraw{difnBdiftE}.
We first substitute \(\E\) and \(\B\) into the equation \refeqraw{diftBdifnE} from equations \refeqraw{plane-wave-E} and
    \refeqraw{plane-wave-B}
    \begin{equation*}
        \pdif{t}\phi^{\B}(\xi) = \pdif{x}\phi^{\E}(\xi), \\
    \end{equation*}
    which evaluates to
    \begin{equation*}
        - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0}}\odv{\phi^{\B}}{\xi} = \odv{\phi^{\E}}{\xi}, \\
    \end{equation*}
    and so with the reminder that \(\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0} = 1/v\), we can conclude that
    \begin{equation}
        \labeq{phiB-phiE-rel}
        \phi^{\B}(\xi) = -\frac{1}{v}\odv{\phi^{\E}}{\xi},
    \end{equation}
    where the constant additive coefficient was omitted similarly as before, because we are deriving only the dynamic
    relations and are not interested in the electro-static and magneto-static fields, since they are not bearers of
    energy.

Putting everything together using equations \refeqraw{E-plane-wave}, \refeqraw{B-plane-wave} and 
    \refeqraw{phiB-phiE-rel}, we get the \ac{EM} plane wave 
    \begin{gather}
        \Escal_y(\vec{r}, t) = F\ab(x - \frac{t}{\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0}}), \\
        \Bscal_z(\vec{r}, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0}}F\ab(x - \frac{t}{\sqrt{\permittivity\permeability_0}}),
    \end{gather}
    where \(F \coloneqq \phi^{\E}_{\rightarrow}\) is a chosen function.

A similar note must be made about the validity of this solution as in \refsubsec{spherical-wave}.
Although it is a solution to Maxwell's equations (\refeqshort{maxwell-simplified}) unlike the spherical wave, there is 
    no physical source that can generate this exact solution.
A possible non-physical source would be an infinite plane that changes its charge.
We use this solution as an approximation, and a good one undeniably, which upholds the wave-front shape when the 
    source is far from the assessed location (the far-field region) and the propagating wave-front can endure its
    strength as in the solution, if we are simultaneously far from the source and we asses the wave-front over a small
    distance relative to the distance of the source.
