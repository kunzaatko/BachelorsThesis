\section{Historical Context}%
\label{historical-context}

Light was perceived in many different ways throughout the history of human kind.

The first major work that studied optics was written by \nameemph{Euclid} in the 4\textsuperscript{th} century BCE.
It mainly dealt with the geometry of vision and did not study the physical aspects of light in much detail. 
However his views of light and vision were significantly different from ours\sidenote{%
        For instance, he conjectured that vision is formed by rays emanating from the observers eyes.
    }.
Optics as a field is often considered to have been commenced by \nameemph{Ḥasan Ibn al-Haytham}\sidenote{%
        More commonly known by the latinized name in Europe as \nameemph{Alhazen}.
    } in the 11\textsuperscript{th} century by his experimental work and 
    the \emph{Book of Optics}, which he wrote to summarize them.
It introduced the idea that vision results from light entering our eyes through the retina as opposed to the other way
    around\sidenote{This was further developed by \nameemph{Johannes Kepler} in the 17\textsuperscript{th} century, who 
    suggested the concept of retinal images.}.
He also extensively studied the \emph{camera obscura} phenomenon in his treatise \emph{On the Shape of the Eclipse} in
    which he ventured to explain the crescent shaped images formed during an eclipse when light passes through a small 
    aperture as can be seen in \reffig{crescent-shaped-image} \sidecite{raynaud2016}.

There have been many attempts to explain light during the middle ages, but more often than not, they were not in 
    accordance with its known experimental properties.
% TODO: Reference (unspecified??) <08-11-23> 
In particular, a notable idea was brought forward by \nameemph{René Descartes} in the beginning of the
    17\textsuperscript{th} century, who described light as a propagating pressure in an unspecified medium\sidenote{This
        view has interestingly close properties to the way, light is described in present day optics.}. 
In 1690 \nameemph{Christiaan Huygens} introduces the \emph{wave theory of light} in his work \emph{Traité de la
    Lumière}.
His major contribution was the conceptualization of light as a vibration in a medium and his methods of analysis are 
    used even today termed as the \emph{Huygens-Fresnel principle}.
The first comprehensive theory of light that was widely adopted, can be dated back to \nameemph{Sir Isaac Newton} at the
    start of the 18\textsuperscript{th} century. %  with regards to the physical reality around us, that was developed in parallel with experimentation
\nameemph{Newton} was aware of the wave theory of light but rejected it in favour of the \emph{corpuscular theory}, 
    which he laid the foundations to\sidecite{newton1704}.
It states that light is composed of particles called \emph{corpuscles} travelling in a straight line with finite
    velocity.
By the rise of the corpuscular theory, the branch that we now call \emph{geometrical optics}, which was earlier 
    developed by \nameemph{Alhazen} was markedly improved.
The corpuscular theory and geometrical optics explained a mayor part of the conundrums that astronomers had about the 
    optical instrumentation of that time and due to this it prevailed for a long time at the forefront of established 
    theories of light.

However, for the recognition of the way light passes through and transforms in a microscope, or in fact any optical 
    system that we use for observation, and especially to study the resolution that is possible to achieve, it is
    necessary to advance to the later centuries and developments made in the wave 
    theory of light.
A radical shift towards the supremacy of the wave model explanation instead of light rays, was the famous 
    \emph{double-slit experiment} performed by \nameemph{Thomas Young} in 1801, which leads to results that are in
    conflict with the sole explanation by the simpler corpuscular theory.
Further mathematical structure to the notion of interference emerging from the double-slit experiment was given by 
    \nameemph{Augustin-Jean Fresnel}, who in 1818 was the first to give a satisfactory explanation of diffraction by a 
    straight edge in the form of the \emph{Fresnel integral}, which will be important for us in further sections.
The brilliant mathematician \nameemph{James Clerk Maxwell} later in 1873 gave a unified and robust theoretical framework
    for \ac{EM} fields, which light is a manifestation of.

\section[Wave Theory of Light]{Foundations of the Wave Theory of Light}%
\labsec{wave-theory-of-light}
% TODO: Explanations of ∇ × and ∇ ⋅ in the margin with Einstein's summation (nabla is also called del...)<06-01-24> 

Maxwell's unification of the theory of \ac{EM} fields came in the manner of twenty differential equations which were 
    later condensed by \nameemph{Oliver Heaviside} into the notorious forms of the four equations that are today known 
    as \emph{Maxwell's equations}.
Given in their most general differential form in \acs{SI} units, they are
    \begin{align}
        \nabla \cdot \permittivity \E &= \rho, \tag{\text{Gauss's law for \(\E\)}}\labeq{gausses-law-E}\\ 
        \nabla \cdot \permeability \B &= 0, \tag{\text{Gauss's law for \(\B\)}}\labeq{gausses-law-B}\\  
        \nabla \times \E &= -\pdif{t}\B, \tag{\text{Faraday's law}} \labeq{faradays-law} \\
        \nabla \times \B &= \permeability \J  + \permittivity \permeability \pdif{t}\E, \tag{\text{Ampère-Maxwell's law}} \labeq{amperes-law}
    \end{align}
    where \(\E=\E(\vec{r}, t)\) and \(\B=\B(\vec{r}, t)\) are the electric and magnetic fields respectively, 
    \(\J=\J(\vec{r}, t)\) is the current density, \(\rho = \rho(\vec{r})\) is the electric charge density, \(\permittivity = 
    \permittivity(\vec{r})\) and \(\permeability = \permeability(\vec{r})\) are permittivity and permeability of the 
    propagation medium respectively.
These equations can be simplified for the case of the medium that is applicable to us \sidecite{goodman2005c}.
As a first simplification, we assume the absence of free charges (\(\rho \equiv 0\)) and free currents (\(\J \equiv 0\)) 
    which lead to the simplification of \refeqraw{gausses-law-E} and \refeqraw{amperes-law}.
We postulate a well-behaved \emph{insulating} (\emph{dielectric}) medium in its response to the electric field, where 
    \(\D = \permittivity \E + \Pol\) is the electric displacement field and \(\Pol\) is the field of polarization, in 
    the form of an assumption of \emph{linearity} implying that the polarization \(\Pol\) is proportional to the 
    electric field, that is \(\Pol \propto \E\), \emph{non-dispersiveness} meaning that the polarization \(\Pol(t)\) is 
    dependent on \(\E(t)\) only at the same time instant, \emph{local homogeneity} meaning that the dependence of 
    \(\Pol\) on \(\E\) is the same at every point \(\vec{r}\) within a neighbourhood and \emph{isotropy} meaning that 
    the relation is identical for every direction of \(\E\). 
In the terms of a medium within which the light propagates \emph{non-dispersiveness} implies that
    \(\permittivity(\lambda) \equiv~\text{const.}\) where \(\lambda\) is the wavelength of the propagating \ac{EM} wave,
    \emph{homogeneity} implies \(\permittivity(\vec{r}) \equiv~\text{const.}\) in some neighbourhood and \emph{isotropy}
    implies that \(\permittivity({\E}/{|\E|}) \equiv~\text{const.}\) so in all directions of the field, the permitivity is
    the same.
Also the medium in optics is assumed to be \emph{non-magnetic} and so \(\permeability \equiv \permeability_0\), which is
    the permeability in vacuum.
The applicable simplified equations are
    \begin{equation}
        \labeq{maxwell-simplified}
        \begin{aligned}
            \nabla \cdot  \E &= 0, &   \nabla \cdot  \B &= 0, \\  
            \nabla \times \E &= -\pdif{t}\B, & \nabla \times \B &= \permittivity \permeability_0 \pdif{t}\E.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
Notice that due to the \emph{homogeneity}, the permittivity \(\permittivity\) and permeability \(\permeability\), no
    longer appear in Gauss's law for \(\E\) and \(\B\) in the simplified equations.
